  I have always been fascinated by time: physical time, subjective time, and the strange fact that we can perceive, feel, remember, and conceptualize something so elusive, yet so central to how we interact with the world.

  I defended my PhD in May 2022 at Aix-Marseille University, where I studied how humans represent temporal order, with a particular focus on the *mental timeline* — the idea that time-related concepts, such as past and future, may be spatially organized from left to right. Drawing on frameworks such as neural reuse and Hebbian learning, I investigated whether the sensorimotor system plays a functional role in temporal cognition. To address this question, I developed experimental protocols combining behavioural measures, mouse tracking, eye tracking, and large-scale online studies involving more than a thousand participants. Overall, this work suggested that temporal concepts are not purely abstract symbols, but may partly rely on sensorimotor experience.

  After my PhD, I joined Virginie van Wassenhove’s group at NeuroSpin, Paris-Saclay, as a postdoctoral researcher involved in the [EXPERIENCE Project](https://experience-project.eu). There, I moved from the representation of temporal order to the perception and neural coding of durations. In one project, I combined immersive virtual reality and EEG to examine how spatial environments shape duration production. Participants produced short intervals in virtual rooms varying in size and ceiling height. Behaviourally, larger environments led to longer produced durations. Using multivariate analyses of EEG signals, we further found that both the produced duration and the spatial context could be decoded from neural activity around the onset of duration production. These results suggest that environmental constraints can influence temporal judgments very early in the unfolding of action.

  In a second project, I investigated whether subjective durations are organized along a simple mental timeline or according to a richer representational geometry. To do so, I combined behavioural similarity judgments and EEG recordings, and used Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) to compare empirical representational dissimilarity matrices with theoretical models. This work suggests that subjective duration is not simply arranged along a linear axis, but follows a multidimensional geometry combining magnitude, context, and periodic-like structure.

  I am now a Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions Global Postdoctoral Fellow, working between the Laboratoire de Psychologie et NeuroCognition (LPNC, Université Grenoble Alpes, CNRS), with [Nathan Faivre](https://nfaivre.netlify.app/#head), and Wake Forest University School of Medicine, with Dr. Kenneth Kishida. My current project investigates the multiscale mechanisms of subjective time and temporal distortions, by bridging behavioural, electrophysiological, and electrochemical approaches. In particular, I aim to combine surface and intracranial electrophysiology with voltammetry to better understand how neural dynamics and dopaminergic fluctuations contribute to the way humans perceive duration.

  More broadly, my goal is to develop an integrative research program on subjective time, linking cognitive models, representational geometry, neural dynamics, and neurochemical mechanisms. I am especially interested in how temporal experiences are structured, transformed, and distorted, both in typical cognition and in clinical conditions where time perception may be altered, such as Parkinson’s disease, schizophrenia, or ADHD.
